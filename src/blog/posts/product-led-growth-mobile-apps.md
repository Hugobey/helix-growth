There's a conversation I keep having with mobile app founders.

They tell me they need more users. More traffic. Better ads. A bigger acquisition budget.

When I ask them what happens after someone installs their app, the conversation often becomes a lot less clear.

And that's where I think the problem starts.

**Most founders are trying to solve a product problem with a marketing solution.**

Don't get me wrong. Acquisition matters. You need people to discover your product before anything else can happen.

But acquiring users is not the same thing as building a growing business.

And if your entire growth strategy depends on continuously bringing new people through the door, you're building a business that needs to keep paying for its own oxygen.

I believe there's a better way to approach mobile app growth.

## Marketing-led vs product-led growth: two ways to build a business

I like to think about products through two broad growth models.

### 1. Marketing-led growth

In a marketing-led business, acquisition is the primary growth driver.

The company invests in advertising, partnerships, influencers, SEO or other distribution channels to bring users in.

The product needs to convert well enough to make that acquisition profitable, but the growth engine largely sits outside the product.

The logic is straightforward:

More budget → More installs → More customers → More revenue.

This model can work extremely well. Some products have excellent acquisition economics, strong distribution advantages and enough market demand to scale through paid channels.

But it comes with a dependency.

When acquisition becomes more expensive, creative performance deteriorates or a distribution channel stops working, growth can become much harder to sustain.

You're constantly feeding the machine.

### 2. Product-led growth

In a product-led business, the product itself plays a much bigger role in driving growth.

Acquisition still matters, but the product is designed to generate more value from every user who discovers it.

Users understand the value quickly. They develop habits. They come back. They upgrade. They invite others. They talk about their experience.

The logic becomes:

More relevant users → More product value → Better retention → More referrals → More sustainable revenue.

The product isn't just where users consume what marketing promised.

**The product becomes part of the distribution strategy.**

That's a fundamentally different way to think about growth.

## Your user acquisition budget might not be the bottleneck

Imagine two apps.

Both acquire 10,000 users per month.

The first app has a polished acquisition funnel, impressive ads and a decent onboarding experience. But users don't develop a strong reason to return. They subscribe, cancel, or simply disappear.

The second app has a less sophisticated acquisition operation. Its ads might even be mediocre.

But once users get inside, something clicks.

They discover value quickly. They develop a reason to come back. They experience small moments of satisfaction that make the product feel worth keeping. Some even share it with friends.

<!-- figure: two-apps -->

Which business has the stronger growth foundation?

I'd argue the second one.

Not because acquisition is irrelevant, but because its product creates more leverage from the attention it receives.

And this is something founders often underestimate.

You don't necessarily need millions of impressions to build a successful mobile app. You need a product that can turn a relatively small number of acquired users into valuable, long-term customers.

Of course, that doesn't mean a poor acquisition strategy can be ignored indefinitely. You still need distribution. But improving the product can make every acquisition channel more effective.

## App conversion is only the beginning

When founders think about product optimization, they often focus on conversion touchpoints.

The [onboarding](/case-studies/prose). The [paywall](/case-studies/perifit). The pricing. The subscription screen.

These are important. Every unnecessary step, confusing message or poorly communicated benefit can cost you revenue.

But I think this is only half of the equation.

A high-performing product shouldn't just make it easy for users to subscribe.

It should make them want to stay.

And, ideally, make them want to talk about it.

Think about the products people naturally recommend to their friends.

It's rarely because they had a particularly smooth checkout experience.

It's because the product made them feel something. It solved a recurring problem exceptionally well. It gave them a sense of progress. It entertained them. It surprised them. It became part of their routine or identity.

That's a different level of product experience.

**The best products don't just remove friction. They create reasons to come back.**

This is where psychological and behavioral mechanisms become interesting.

Progress indicators, rewards, anticipation, social proof, personalization, challenges, feedback loops and moments of delight can all influence how people experience a product.

Not as cheap tricks to manipulate users, but as ways to make the value of the product more tangible, engaging and memorable.

A product can be functional and still be forgettable.

The objective is to build something people actually want to return to.

## Retention is the foundation. Referral is the multiplier.

I see retention and referral as two of the most important dimensions of a sustainable product growth strategy.

Retention tells you whether people continue to find value in what you've built.

Referral tells you whether that value is strong enough to travel beyond the original user.

They reinforce each other.

A product that retains users has more opportunities to generate revenue, strengthen habits and create positive experiences.

A product that generates positive experiences has more opportunities to be recommended, shared or discussed.

And referrals can bring in new users who already have some degree of trust or understanding of the product.

This creates a powerful loop:

**Acquisition → Activation → Retention → Referral → Acquisition.**

<!-- figure: growth-loop -->

The stronger the loop, the less dependent the business becomes on constantly increasing its acquisition spend.

There is an important distinction here: retention alone doesn't guarantee a healthy business. You still need monetization, sustainable unit economics and enough acquisition to support your ambitions.

But without retention, you're constantly replacing users instead of building on the value you've already created.

That's an expensive way to grow.

## Stop looking at your dashboard. Start understanding your users.

This is also why I believe product growth requires more than monitoring conversion rates and revenue dashboards.

A dashboard can tell you that 40% of users abandon onboarding.

It won't necessarily tell you why.

Are they confused? Are they unconvinced? Did they expect something different? Are they being asked for information before understanding the value?

The same applies to retention.

A retention curve can show you when users disappear. But understanding their behavior, motivations and experience is what helps you identify opportunities to change that outcome.

You need to investigate the product beyond its surface-level metrics.

Look at how users interact with each screen. Understand where they hesitate, what they engage with, which segments behave differently and where the experience fails to meet expectations.

Then connect those observations to business outcomes.

Sometimes the opportunity is a better onboarding experience.

Sometimes it's a different [monetization strategy](/case-studies/wizz).

Sometimes it's introducing a reason for users to return, or making an existing product benefit much more compelling.

The point isn't to optimize every screen.

It's to identify the changes that create the greatest leverage for the business.
